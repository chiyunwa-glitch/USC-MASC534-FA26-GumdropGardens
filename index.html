    Electron room

    Scanning electron microscopy! A thin beam of electrons sweeps across your candy, and detectors catch the electrons that bounce or splash out. Press Scan and watch the image build up one line at a time, then switch detectors to explore surface shape, composition contrast, or elemental carbon.

    After a scan, hover or drag over the image to move the beam spot.

    Display resolution is how many pixels the image has. Resolving power is the smallest detail the beam and detector can separate, and it does not change when you change pixels. Beam current and dwell time set the noise, charging and resolving power (ref. 22), and noise is added on top of a clean image, so a slower scan removes grain without blurring. A feature is usually visible when SNR is at least 5 (Rose criterion, ref. 8). EDS is a simulated carbon X-ray map of an uncoated sample, because a carbon coat would add its own carbon (ref. 23).     From nano to mouth

    Every taste and texture you notice starts with something tiny. Follow the chain from your recipe, down to the nanoscale structure, and back up to what you would feel in a bite.

    Try 37 °C, the temperature of your mouth.

    State diagram

    Your candy in the mouth

      Try these experiments

      1. Cook a caramel hotter, up to 150 °C. Water drops, Tg climbs above your serving temperature, and it flips from soft to glassy.
      2. Add glucose syrup to a fudge. Watch crystal size and smoothness change.
      3. Whip a candy. Air cuts the bite force but brings flavor sooner.
      4. Raise the serving temperature to 37 °C and see when a hard candy starts to soften.
      Show the equations

      Water activity (Norrish): ln(aₕ/xₕ) = −K(1 − xₕ)², with K = 6.47 for sucrose.

      Mixing the dry solids (Fox): 1/Tg = Σ wᵢ / Tg,ᵢ

      Water as plasticizer (Gordon–Taylor): Tg = (wₛ Tg,ₛ + k wₕ Tg,ₕ) / (wₛ + k wₕ), with k ≈ 5.1 and Tg of water about 136 K. Sugar that crystallizes leaves the glass, which raises the water share in what remains.

      Stiffness of the sugar matrix: log₁₀ E = 5 + 4 / (1 + exp((T − Tg − 5) / 6)) in Pa, plus a gelatin network E_gel ≈ 1.5×10⁴ c² (c in wt%). The shape is a simplified fit, not measured data.

      Crystals (Halpin–Tsai, stiff inclusions): E = Eₘ (1 + 2φc) / (1 − φc). Fat droplets (soft inclusions): E × (1 − φf) / (1 + 1.5 φf). Air (Gibson–Ashby): E × (1 − φp)²

      Bite force: F = min(0.3 E, σ_fracture) × A with A = 10 mm² and σ_fracture capped at 25 MPa for glass.

      Beam penetration: R ≈ 0.0276 A En / (Z0.889 ρ) (Kanaya–Okayama, refs. 7, 8), with R in µm, E in keV, A the mean atomic weight, Z the mean atomic number, ρ in g/cm³, and n ≈ 1.67 above 5 keV (about 1.35 below). For sucrose (A ≈ 13.3, Z ≈ 6.7, ρ ≈ 1.59 g/cm³) that gives about 0.04 µm at 1 kV and about 4 µm at 15 kV, which is why the 15 kV image looks blurrier. Sugar does not conduct, so charge builds up and makes the image bright and streaky. Real samples are coated, imaged at low voltage (about 2–5 kV often works), or imaged with backscattered electrons or low vacuum (refs. 9–13).

      Flavor release (Noyes–Whitney): dm/dt = k A (Cₛ − C), so release time scales as 1/A. Air and tiny crystals raise the surface area A.

      All parameters are simplified and illustrative. Use them to see trends, and check textbook values before quoting numbers.
